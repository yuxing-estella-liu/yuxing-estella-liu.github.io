Yuxing (Estella) Liu

Material programming · 4D printing · ML-driven inverse design · closed-loop robotic fabrication

M.Sc. ITECH, University of Stuttgart
Research Assistant, ICD / IntCDC
Stuttgart, Germany

I study how to program the behaviour of materials and how to build the computational and robotic workflows that make that behaviour designable. My current work uses 4D-printed wood–PLA bilayers that bend when humidity changes. I control that bending through gradient porosity: a designer specifies a target curved surface, and an inverse-design pipeline returns a fabrication-ready print.

I am an M.Sc. student in ITECH at the University of Stuttgart and a graduate research assistant at the Institute for Computational Design and Construction (ICD) and the Cluster of Excellence IntCDC. I work on the ArchiBioFoam bio-based foam project (Horizon Europe EIC Pathfinder), the RAW reclaimed-materials knowledge graph (Horizon Europe), and robotic assembly in IntCDC's Large-Scale Construction Robotics Laboratory (LCRL). Before that I began doctoral research at the Bartlett, UCL, with Prof. Marjan Colletti and Dr. Kostas Grigoriadis, and studied at the University of Pennsylvania (MSD-AAD, GPA 3.84) and Suzhou University of Science and Technology (B.Arch, ranked 1st in cohort).

Of the three links in the chain of material, design and fabrication, the one I care about most is the material. Over the long term I want to learn where machine learning genuinely speeds up that chain: choosing which experiment to run next, replacing slow simulation with surrogate models, and feeding sensor data from fabrication back into the material model.

I am applying to PhD programs for Fall 2027 in material-informed design, digital fabrication and computational design. Research direction ↓

research direction: closed-loop material–structure–process co-optimisation

research question

How can an evolving material library, uncertainty-aware inverse design and sensor-guided fabrication be integrated into a closed-loop framework that jointly optimises material selection, structural design and manufacturing processes, while improving design fidelity through physical validation and feedback?

Gaps

gap 1Fragmented material knowledgeMaterial knowledge sits in literature, databases and lab systems, and new experimental and fabrication results rarely flow back into a reusable, provenance-aware library that supports design.Himanen et al. 2019 · Gupta et al. 2022 · MacLeod et al. 2020 · Szymanski et al. 2023
gap 2Material, structure and fabrication optimised apartInverse design is usually tied to one material system or process. Jointly optimising discrete material choices, material distributions, geometry and process parameters under uncertain properties remains hard.Bessa et al. 2019 · Gladman et al. 2016
gap 3Fabrication feedback stops at the machineIn-situ sensing mostly catches defects or tunes process parameters. It rarely tells whether a deviation came from the material, the model or the process, or changes upstream design decisions.Chen et al. 2024 · Choi et al. 2026
gap 4Uncertain transfer across scalesBehaviour validated on lab specimens may not hold for larger components, where material variability, tolerances and boundary conditions add new uncertainty.Keating et al. 2017 · Cheng et al. 2021

Proposed framework

An experimentally grounded loop. Machine learning comes in where it is the most efficient solution, for extracting material data, choosing the next experiment and replacing slow simulation, not as an end in itself. The first application is sustainable, robotically fabricated composite building components; architecture, engineering and construction is the focus, not the limit, of the method.

failure-informed feedback: new experiments or material re-selection
01 Evolving material library · gap 1Literature, supplier data and lab records plus new formulation and testing, merged into a provenance-aware library with checked units and test conditions. Uncertainty-aware models pick the most informative next experiment. Failed formulations are kept too.so far: SMP Curvature Tool · DeformMatter · ArchiBioFoam · RAW knowledge graph
↓↑ uncertainty triggers informative experiments
02 Material-aware inverse design & co-optimisation · gap 2Physics-constrained generative methods, uncertainty-aware surrogates and mixed discrete–continuous multi-objective optimisation choose material, spatial distribution, geometry and process parameters together, for fidelity, performance, material efficiency and feasibility.so far: Gradient Matter · DeformMatter · Growing Vase
↓↑ inverse-design revision
03 Sensor-guided fabrication & failure-informed re-optimisation · gap 3Real-time sensing, deviation detection and adaptive control during fabrication. Deviations are diagnosed as material, model or process problems and routed to the right fix: more experiments, re-selection, design revision or new process settings.so far: C.U.R.E. · Robotic Knots · The Self-Pleasuring Plant
↓↑ deviation diagnosis
04 AEC demonstration & scale-aware validation · gap 4Staged validation from material specimens to small structural prototypes and a larger component-level demonstrator, testing whether predictions and optimised designs still hold as scale and manufacturing conditions change.so far: specimen-level validation in Gradient Matter and Material In-Formation
validation updates the library and the design models

long-term horizon: material intelligence beyond Earth

Building on the Moon or Mars is the hardest test of the loop above. Every gap gets sharper there: material knowledge is thin, material, structure and process cannot be planned apart, nobody is on site to read a failure, and nothing can be tested at full scale before it is built. If the framework can work there, it will be far more robust here.

01 · evolving material libraryLearning local materials from few samplesRegolith-based and in-situ composites start almost uncharacterised. A provenance-aware library has to grow from a handful of tests, including the ones that fail.
02 · co-optimisationMaterial, structure and process as one decisionWith scarce material and limited machines, the mix, the geometry and the fabrication plan must be chosen together, under uncertain properties, temperature swings and vacuum.
03 · failure-informed feedbackRobots that diagnose their own deviationsWith no operator on site, the system itself must tell a material problem from a model or process error and choose the fix: test again, re-select, redesign or adjust.
04 · scale-aware validationFrom Earth samples to off-world componentsSpecimens and prototypes tested on Earth have to predict components built in another gravity and climate, so transfer across scales and conditions is the core question.

This is a direction I want to grow into during a PhD, not work I have done yet.

  1. ACADIA

    Gradient Matter: Multi-Objective Inverse Design for 4D-Printed Hygromorphs through Gradient Porosity Programming

    Yuxing Liu

    Proceedings of the 46th ACADIA Conference (ACADIA 2026) · accepted, full paper

    details

    Hygromorphic 4D printing enables printed materials to change shape over time in response to moisture, opening possibilities for climate-responsive, resource-efficient building solutions. This research presents an integrated computational framework for the inverse design and multi-objective optimization of 4D-printed wood–PLA hygroscopic bilayers through gradient porosity programming. Unlike forward workflows, it lets users define target curved surfaces and automatically generates fabrication-ready meshes. The method is structured as a cyber-physical loop connecting fabrication-aware geometry generation, empirical material characterization, synthetic data generation, predictive modelling and an integrated inverse solver. It encodes uniform- and variable-curvature morphologies through calibrated gradient porosity fields; experiments show multiple target deformations can be achieved with acceptable error, and that different gradient logics correspond to distinct curvature conditions.

    Controllable Deformation: A 4D-Printing Simulation Framework for Porosity-Driven Hygromorphic Bilayer Structure

    Yuxing Liu, Xuanyu Lu, Marjan Colletti, Kostas Grigoriadis

    Computational Design and Robotic Fabrication (CDRF 2025) · full paper

    details

    This research proposes a 4D-printing simulation framework that uses porosity as a design parameter to program deformation in hygromorphic bilayer structures. Grounded in Timoshenko's sandwich beam theory, the framework integrates physical experimentation, parametric modeling and dynamic simulation in Grasshopper, enabling inverse design of porosity distributions for target bending. Bilayers of wood-based (active) and PLA (passive) filaments show consistent humidity-driven shape change. The study proceeds through physical experiments, dynamic simulation to collect curvature data, and mathematical modeling to refine design guidelines; a prototype façade panel validates the framework, with predicted bending closely matching experiments.

    Yuxing Liu, Hangchuan Wei, Marjan Colletti, Kostas Grigoriadis

    Proceedings of the 30th CAADRIA Conference (CAADRIA 2025) · published

Gradient Matter

Multi-objective inverse design for 4D-printed hygromorphs through gradient porosity programming

Venue
ACADIA 2026, peer-reviewed full paper (first author)
Authors
Yuxing Liu, Yerong Huang, Achim Menges · ICD and IntCDC, University of Stuttgart
Methods
Physical experiments, Timoshenko-based synthetic data, surface-fitted forward equations, multi-objective optimisation (RBFMOpt), physical validation
Materials
Laywood meta5 (active layer) and PLA (passive layer), multi-material FDM
Support
EIC project ARCHIBIOFOAM (Horizon Europe, GA 101161052) and DFG EXC 2120/2 (IntCDC)
18porosity-gradient configurations, 3 repeats each
2.16°–5.14°RMSE of the forward equations
0.1°–2.8°deviation, Pp validation at 24 h

Research question

How can a target curved surface be translated into a printable porosity distribution for a hygroscopic bilayer?

Hygromorphic systems are usually designed forward: choose material and print parameters, print, observe the shape, repeat. This work reverses that. You define the target shape, the solver finds the material and printing parameters, and the design intent becomes fabrication logic.

Material system and design variables

Moisture change makes the wood-filament layer swell or shrink while the PLA layer resists. The differential strain becomes curvature. Three variables shape the porosity field:

  • Pp, passive-layer porosity, sets the baseline.
  • Ga, the gradient amplifier, controls the intensity. A larger Ga means a bigger difference between rows.
  • Gt, the gradient topology, shapes the field: radial outward (0), linear gradual (1) or radial inward (2).

Method: experiments + surrogate modelling + optimisation = inverse solver

  • Physical experiments. 18 porosity-gradient configurations, each printed three times, soaked in water for 12 h and measured after 24 h and 72 h of drying. These are the empirical anchor points.
  • Physics-supported surrogate. Timoshenko bilayer theory generates synthetic parameter sweeps around the anchors. Fitted response surfaces give fast, topology-specific forward equations (RMSE 2.16°–5.14°).
  • Inverse solver. Target surface → panel targets by segmentation → search over Ga and Gt with the forward equations → Pareto selection → export of fabrication-ready panel meshes. The objectives (RBFMOpt) minimise angle error, maximise variation between Ga values and minimise material usage.

Validation

Target, predicted and measured angles were compared after fabrication. Measured deviations were:

  • at 24 h: 0.1°–2.8° for Pp validation (Gt = 0) and 0.01°–5.6° for Ga validation (Gt = 2). The linear gradual mode (Gt = 1) deviated by 30° or more.
  • at 72 h: 1.1°–3.7° (Pp), 1.2°–2.2° (Ga), 1.2°–5.7° (Gt) and 0.6°–6.1° for complex geometry.

The linear gradual mode did not fit simple arcs, but it made complex geometries with several bending angles possible.

What remains open

The next step is two-dimensional, double-curvature surfaces, where porosity gradients work in several directions, plus a second, physics-based model (for example agent-based) for more flexibility. The workflow is being packaged as the DeformMatter Grasshopper plug-in.

Porosity-Programmed Hygromorphic Bilayers

From qualitative experiments to a calibrated simulation framework for self-folding 4D-printed structures

Context
MPhil/PhD research, the Bartlett, UCL (2024–25), with Prof. Marjan Colletti and Dr. Kostas Grigoriadis
Papers
CAADRIA 2025 (with Hangchuan Wei) · CDRF 2025 (with Xuanyu Lu)
Methods
Wood-filament / PLA bilayer printing, humidity actuation, Timoshenko sandwich-beam theory, Grasshopper dynamic simulation, regression modelling

Stage 1: what porosity does (CAADRIA 2025)

Three experiments separated the roles of porosity in the active wood layer (Pa) and the passive PLA layer (Pp):

  • active-layer porosity mainly controls response speed;
  • passive-layer porosity mainly controls deformation magnitude;
  • a balanced porosity ratio Rp between the layers gives controlled, predictable transformation.

Three envelope prototypes showed how humidity-driven shading could adapt with no energy input and less material.

Stage 2: making it predictable (CDRF 2025, Controllable Deformation)

Building on Timoshenko's bilayer theory, a simulation framework links physical tests, parametric modelling and dynamic simulation, so porosity distributions can be designed inversely for a target bending angle. A prototype façade panel validated the framework, with predicted and measured bending closely matched. This work led directly to the multi-objective inverse design in Gradient Matter.

C.U.R.E.

Collaborative UR–KUKA resin extrusion for metal bonding

Context
ITECH · Computational Design and Digital Fabrication 2026, University of Stuttgart
Team
Yunseo Chang, Tuğçe Naz Demir, Yuxing Liu, Julia Richard
Robots
UR10 with gripper (reach 1,300 mm, payload 10 kg, ±0.1 mm) · KUKA KR 30 HA with extruder (reach 2,033 mm, payload 30 kg, ±0.05 mm)
Tools
KRL, URScript, Grasshopper (SuperSerial, KRC Write, TCP client), Arduino, piezo and ToF sensors, UV-curable resin

Research question

Can multi-robot collaboration provide a repeatable, operator-free fabrication method for precise, clean, high-strength metal bonding in construction?

Welding and bonding by hand expose workers to toxic fumes, vary widely in quality and are checked only afterwards. An autonomous cell can work in isolation, with millimetre precision and real-time tracking. We used UV-curable resin as a stand-in for welding. It also brings joint flexibility, surface continuity, lower weight and faster assembly.

Gripper on the UR

A Dynamixel MX64 servo drives a rack-and-pinion mechanism for symmetric jaws. A piezo sensor listens for the vibration peak when the tube touches the structure. The UR then stops and the Grasshopper host triggers the KUKA program. Gripper states: IDLE, GRABBED, LISTEN.

Resin extruder on the KUKA

A NEMA17 stepper drives a resin syringe through a flexible coupling, with a UV pen and a ToF distance sensor (I²C). When the tube sits at 40 ± 2 mm, the motor turns 10° (6 steps, about 5 drops in 0.2 s), and the UV LED cures the joint for 1 minute before the loop repeats.

Communication and path planning

One Grasshopper instance reads both microcontrollers through SuperSerial, turns sensor values into booleans and writes two KRL variables: SRC_TRIGGER from the piezo sensor and EXTRUSION_TRIGGER from the ToF sensor. The UR runs moveL / halt / wait over a TCP client, and the KUKA runs LIN / wait in its .src program.

Challenges and outlook

  • Sensor values were hard to keep consistent, and simulated plane orientations did not always match the KUKA.
  • Next: update joint positions live to absorb tolerances, and move communication to an MQTT or ROS broker for low-latency multi-robot sync.

The Self-Pleasuring Plant

A "useless machine": a plant that reacts to your touch by touching itself until it is satisfied

Context
ITECH · Computational Design and Digital Fabrication, useless machine assignment, University of Stuttgart, 2026
Team
Julia Richard, Nicole Agnese D'Apolito, Yuxing Liu
Hardware
Arduino UNO, leaf electrodes with amplifier modules, servo motor (later a continuous FS5109R), LCD1602 display
Build guide
The Self-Pleasuring Plant on Instructables (7 steps)

Concept

A useless machine exists only to answer its own trigger. Here a houseplant gets excited when you touch it, searches for and touches itself, then shows its satisfaction, three times in a row before it goes to sleep.

How it works

  • Input, touch. Electrodes on the leaves pick up the touch signal, amplified by the amplifier of a sound-sensor module and read in the Arduino IDE. The code sets a baseline at start-up and compares new readings against it dynamically.
  • Movement, motor. A signal above the baseline threshold starts the motor, which turns and pulls a leaf.
  • Reaction, self-touch. When the pulled leaf touches the second electrode, the motor stops and turns back.
  • Output, LCD. A "satisfied" message appears, the system resets and the leaf returns to its original position.

The machine moves through five states: idle, trigger, self-touch, rejection (an interruption while it is busy) and sleep after three cycles.

Challenges and next steps

  • The first motor was too weak. A continuous FS5109R motor pulled several leaves smoothly.
  • The leaves' electrical signal is weak, so the amplifiers and code needed careful calibration. Proper amplifier modules would replace the modified sound-sensor boards.
  • Next: a sound output so the plant can say "woo-aah".

Material In-Formation

Curvature prediction in self-shaping cross-laminated timber bilayers using agent-based modelling

Context
ITECH · Agent-Based Modelling (ABxM) seminar, University of Stuttgart, 2026
Team
Tuğçe Naz Demir, Yuxing Liu
Reference
Grönquist et al. (2019), Science Advances 5(9), eaax1311; Urbach Tower, HygroShell, HygroShape
Tools
Rhino / Grasshopper, ABxM.Core framework (C#)
9,000voxel agents across two configurations
1.7%–4.3%curvature deviation from the published experiments

Question

How can moisture transport, grain direction, layer identity and local stress rules produce a deforming bilayer? The reference work used a top-down process (FEA in ABAQUS plus the Timoshenko equation). We built a bottom-up, emergent one in which local material behaviour produces global form, at a lighter computing cost than FEA.

Agent system

We compared three systems (node agents only, voxel agents only, and hierarchical) and chose the hierarchical one: each voxel centre is an agent and its 8 corner nodes are shared sub-agents. Node-only systems are too heavy, and voxel-only systems conflict because voxels share no nodes.

  • Static data: start position, layer identity (active or passive), grain direction (R/T/L), 6 neighbours.
  • Dynamic data: relative humidity (RH), wood moisture content (WMC), stress, strain and node planes.
  • Each tick (10 hours, 90 ticks for 900 hours of drying): WMC changes → desired shrinkage per grain direction → mismatch with actual strain → stress (Hooke's law) → forces on shared nodes → nodes relax → the voxel geometry updates.

Validation

Against the published experiments, curvature deviated by 3.1% and 1.7% for the two beech configurations and 4.3% and 2.3% for spruce. A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis (±20% per parameter) shows which material properties drive the result.

Growing Vase

Surrogate-assisted single- and multi-objective optimisation of a printable, functional vase

Context
ITECH · Computational Exploration seminar, SoSe 2026, ICD/CA (Department for Computing in Architecture)
Team
Fabian Opitz, Yunseo Chang, Yuxing Liu
Tutors
Max Zorn, Luisa Claus, Prof. Dr. Thomas Wortmann
Methods
Sobol sampling, Spearman correlation, k-means clustering, supervised surrogates (Extra Trees, XGBoost, MLP), CMA-ES / RBFOpt / TPE, RBFMOpt / NSGA-II / HypE
2,000Sobol-sampled designs
R² 0.996Extra Trees surrogate for the water centre of gravity
−13%optimisation runtime

Research question

How can we generate a printable and functional vase geometry that a given type of bouquet, with a given number of flowers, can fit into?

Parametric design makes personal products possible, but it is hard to be sure a design can be printed and also works, because the design space is huge and unpredictable. Each design takes three inputs: the bouquet configurations, a standard 500 ml of water, and the geometric variables.

Three conflicting objectives

  • Printability. Surface normals are compared with the Z axis. Faces at 0°–45° or 135°–180° count as overhangs, and a height-weighted exponential penalty is strictest near the base, where watertightness matters most.
  • Collision detection. The stem area of each bouquet, × 4 for breathing room, is subtracted from the opening area. The worst bouquet is minimised, so the smaller ones fit too.
  • Tipping. The combined centre of gravity of water and leaning flowers is projected onto the base. Its distance to the base outline, for the worst bouquet, is passed through exp(0.1x) − 1 so that a tipping design is penalised hard.

Optimising printability alone gave slender, small-based vases that could hold a single flower. That is why the two functional objectives were added.

Learning from 2,000 designs

  • Sampling. A Sobol sequence gave 2,000 evenly spread, reproducible designs for both the analysis and surrogate training.
  • Correlation. Sine-wave frequency drives printability (ρ = −0.92: higher frequency, smaller penalty) and the radius amplifier drives stability (ρ = −0.68). Collision correlates only weakly with anything (base radius, ρ ≈ 0.23).
  • Clustering. The design variables do not cluster (silhouette ≈ 0.11–0.12), but the objectives fall into four performance regimes (k = 4, silhouette ≈ 0.47).
  • Surrogate. Linear regression, random forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost, MLP and Extra Trees were compared for the water-fill calculation. Extra Trees was best (R² 0.996 for the water CoG, RMSE ≈ 2.75 mm). X and Y were hard to predict, so the final model predicts only the water height (R² ≈ 0.99). It runs in a Grasshopper Python component and cut the runtime of a 100-sample benchmark by about 13%.

Benchmarking the optimisers

  • Single objective (printability, 1,000 evaluations per run). RBFOpt found the near-optimum within 50 evaluations. TPE (Bayesian) caught up after about 450 evaluations. CMA-ES varied widely between runs.
  • Multi-objective. RBFMOpt clearly beat NSGA-II and HypE on hypervolume and covered the Pareto front more densely. The runs also exposed trade-offs: collision vs tipping (r = −0.48), and frequency vs printability (r = −0.55) and stability (r = −0.54).
  • Archetypes from the front. Least tipping (NSGA-II): bottom-heavy, very wide base. Least collision (RBFMOpt): a wide flared opening. Optimal trade-off (RBFMOpt): moderate undulations and base, with an opening wide enough for standard bouquets.

Against a simple cone, and the printed result

Compared with an inverted cone, both shapes print and both fit the bouquets, but the cone tips over because its wide top pushes the centre of gravity out. The optimised vase keeps it inside the base. The final design was printed on a Bambu textured PEI plate. The top rim had a print flaw caused by the strict penalty function, which became one of the lessons.

Takeaways

  • Penalty functions decide how the algorithm trades objectives; small miscalibrations show up as physical flaws.
  • Choosing the right complexity for a surrogate is hard: accuracy and speed pull against each other.
  • Variable ranges set at the start limit what any optimiser can find. Next: adaptive penalties for FDM, hybrid or deep-learning surrogates, and dynamic parameter bounds.

Halite Hopper Crystals

A growth system that combines particle clustering with procedural geometry to simulate halite hopper crystals

Context
ITECH · Computational Design Techniques and Design Thinking (ICD, Prof. Achim Menges), WS 2025/26
Team
Phoebe Milda Kleinau, Tuğçe Naz Demir, Yuxing Liu
Methods
Diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA), biased random walk, procedural hopper geometry, Python 3 in Grasshopper

Phenomenon

Hopper crystals form under high supersaturation. NaCl ions sit in a cubic lattice, and growth is edge-dominated: new layers start before the previous ones are complete. The result is hollow cores and stepped, terraced faces.

DLA growth algorithm

  • A high-resolution cubic domain where each cell has 26 neighbours, which gives the random walker 26 degrees of freedom and sets up collision detection.
  • Biased Brownian motion: walkers move toward the centre 70% of the time and randomly 30%.
  • Walkers spawn at random around the seed and attach with a density-based "supersaturation" probability, then stick with a random rotation.

Hopper crystal formation

  • Stepped pyramids: 8 terraced levels on each cube face, each with a flat tread and a vertical riser.
  • Inverted tapering with a decay formula, and parametric hollowing with a 0.8 depth factor.
  • Age-based sizing: older crystals are larger, from 3× down to 1×.

The component takes a domain box, seed point, grid spacing, number of crystals and stick probability, and outputs the geometry and points. The printed model measures about 6 × 7 × 8 cm.

Outlook

The DLA algorithm reproduces the clustering of halite well, and the age logic creates a natural hierarchy. Next steps are a hollow-shell logic to make the model easier to print, and linking stick probability to real-world data such as temperature.

DeformMatter

Grasshopper plug-in · documentation · work in progress

What it does

A wood–PLA bilayer bends as the wood layer changes moisture. How far it bends depends on the porosity printed into it. DeformMatter works backwards from a target shape. It splits a surface into panels, finds the passive-layer porosity and gradient amplifier that give each panel its target bending angle, and prepares the multi-material print.

Components

01 Design

  • Target Surface PanelsSplits a surface into panels and reads each panel's target bending angle.
  • Fabrication-Clamped PorosityBuilds the flat porous panels, with porosity limited to what can be printed.
  • Bending PreviewShows the panels bent to their predicted angles.

02 Predict

  • Material LibraryActive and passive material properties, extendable with a JSON file.
  • Experimental DataImports bending measurements from CSV or XLSX.
  • Calibration SettingsLayer thicknesses, widths, stiffness and swelling defaults.
  • Physics CalibrationFits a Timoshenko-based bilayer model to the measurements.
  • Synthetic SweepSamples the calibrated model by grid, Latin hypercube or Sobol sequence.
  • Surrogate FittingFits a forward model to experimental and synthetic data together.

03 Inverse Design

  • Design ObjectivesMaterial usage, amplifier difference between neighbouring panels, angle tolerance.
  • Inverse Panel SolverBounded stochastic Pareto search for each panel's porosity and gradient settings.
  • Solution SelectionBrowse the feasible designs and output the chosen flat panels.

04 Fabricate

  • Fabrication SettingsPore geometry, divisions, nozzle size, layer thickness and layer counts.
  • Print Path GeneratorMulti-material print paths: zigzag, parallel, grid, concentric or contours.
  • Path VisualizerSteps through the print layer by layer with a moving nozzle.
  • GCode GeneratorMarlin-style G-code with tool changes, returned as text.

Basic workflow

  1. Connect a surface to Target Surface Panels.
  2. Send the panels and target angles to the Inverse Panel Solver, together with Fabrication Settings and Design Objectives. Pick the drying state (24 h or 72 h), the allowed gradient modes, the search ranges and the iterations, then set Run to True.
  3. Choose a design in Solution Selection and check it in Bending Preview.
  4. Generate paths with the Print Path Generator, inspect them in the Path Visualizer, and export with the GCode Generator.

Built-in forward model

By default the solver uses equations fitted to my physical experiments, one for each drying state (24 h, 72 h) and gradient mode. They predict the bending angle from passive porosity and gradient amplifier for the tested reference panel. Use the Predict components to build new models from new measurements.

Current limits

  • The built-in equations describe the tested reference panels. Changing panel length changes the geometry only.
  • Panels must be rectangular and developable.
  • Geometry, viewport behaviour, slicing and G-code motion have not been verified inside Rhino yet. Check every print before running it.

SMP Curvature Tool

Computer-vision analysis of paired before/after SMP samples · documentation

What it does

Shape-memory polymer strips are printed flat, programmed and then recover a curved shape. Measuring the curvature of dozens of samples by hand is slow and inconsistent. This tool reads the photos, finds each strip and reports its radius and curvature in millimetres, with an overlay so every result can be checked.

How it measures

  1. Pairing. Photos are matched by sample tag, for example T4B8_1, and grouped by the part before the underscore (T4B8).
  2. Tag reading. The batch app reads the paper tag in each photo with OCR and proposes a new file name for you to confirm.
  3. Calibration. Metric ticks on the ruler in each image set the millimetre scale, read from horizontal or vertical ruler bands. If a ruler is unreadable, the image is flagged and the batch median scale is used. Manual and known-length calibration are available as explicit overrides.
  4. Segmentation. The strip is separated from the background, the ruler and the tag.
  5. Curvature. A least-squares circle is fitted to the deformed boundary. The radius R is reported in mm and the curvature is κ = 1 / R in mm⁻¹.
  6. Results. Boundary overlays, a CSV and JSON of all samples, group averages and plots, and an export into the 4D-printing curvature Excel template.

Worked example: sample G1_S3

  1. The ruler ticks give the scale: 0.0329 mm per pixel.
  2. The flat strip measures 58.80 mm long before deformation.
  3. Boundary points of the curled strip are fitted with a circle by least squares: radius R = 10.82 mm, fit error (RMSE) 0.19 mm.
  4. Curvature is the inverse of the radius: κ = 1 / R = 0.0924 mm⁻¹.

Batch app workflow

  1. Double-click run_app.bat and select the after-deformation photos from the camera's SD card.
  2. Choose an empty output folder, check the OCR rename preview and enter the sample dimensions for the batch.
  3. Click 1 Rename, then 2 Calculate to see the results table, then 3 Evaluate for group averages and plots.
  4. Export the selected plots, or all of them, as PNG files.

The source photos are never copied. The output folder holds only the renamed working images, results/ (overlays, CSV, JSON) and analysis/ (exported plots).

Command line

  • python smp_curvature_tool.py --measure-only --overwrite measures every sample group; add --groups to pick groups.
  • python smp_curvature_tool.py --export-template-only fills the Excel template from the measurements.
  • python smp_curvature_tool.py --reset-test clears generated results before another test.

Always check the boundary overlays before trusting the numbers: if an overlay follows a bright patch instead of the strip, review that image.
